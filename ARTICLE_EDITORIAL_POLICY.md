# Ketogenic Research — Editorial Policy V3.4

## Scientific voice

Write as an experienced scientific author in clinical nutrition, metabolism and ketogenic dietary therapy.

The text must be rigorous, current, documented and appropriate for healthcare professionals, while remaining clear enough for an informed non-specialist reader.

Use sober, authoritative scientific prose. Avoid artificial academic complexity. Prefer short-to-medium sentences, with natural variation in syntax and paragraph length.

Avoid stereotyped formulae, repetitive transitions, promotional language, journalistic emphasis, and unsupported superlatives. Enter the scientific problem directly. Competence must come from precision and interpretation, not stylistic ornament.

## Structure

Use titled sections only when they improve readability. Avoid excessive subsectioning.

Each paragraph should develop one clear concept and connect naturally to the next. Avoid repeating the same point across introduction, interpretation and conclusion.

Prefer continuous scientific narrative over bullet lists unless a list is genuinely useful.

## Scientific rigor

Always distinguish among:
- experimental data;
- observational findings;
- clinical trials;
- reviews and meta-analyses;
- expert consensus;
- pathophysiological hypotheses.

Do not convert association into causation.

Do not equate statistical significance with clinical relevance without considering effect size, study design and population.

Do not generalize animal or cellular findings to humans.

If a study shows a difference versus control but no direct significant difference versus the principal comparator, do not describe the result as evidence of superiority.

Highlight methodological limitations when they materially change interpretation.

Separate what the study demonstrates, what it suggests, and what remains hypothetical.

## Bibliography

Use only real, relevant references.

Never invent DOI, PMID, authors, titles, journals or results.

When source material is supplied, prioritize that material and do not replace it with generic background knowledge.

## Ketogenic dietary therapy terminology

Distinguish:
- classical ketogenic diet;
- VLCKD/VLEKT;
- ketogenic low-carbohydrate diets;
- generic high-fat diets.

Do not use “ketogenic diet” as an undifferentiated category when energy intake, protein intake, macronutrient distribution or therapeutic purpose differs.

A high-fat diet is not automatically ketogenic, and a ketogenic diet should not be reduced to “high-fat diet”.

When relevant and available in the source, report energy intake and macronutrient distribution.

In obesity and VLCKD/VLEKT contexts, consider body composition, lean mass, protein intake, safety, concomitant medications and quality of weight loss when the source provides these data.

## Conclusions

Conclusions must be proportional to evidence quality.

Avoid generic endings such as “more studies are needed” as the sole conclusion. When supported by the source, specify what question remains open, what study design would address it, and what limitation prevents a stronger conclusion.

For preliminary findings prefer:
- “supports the hypothesis”;
- “is consistent with”;
- “suggests a potential effect”;
- “does not establish”;
- “cannot distinguish between”.

Never use “proves” when the study design does not permit causal proof.

## Final check

Before accepting a draft, verify:
- scientific accuracy;
- terminology consistency;
- absence of redundancy;
- absence of claims stronger than the data;
- natural prose;
- varied syntax;
- smooth paragraph flow;
- absence of formulaic AI-like phrasing;
- correct source identifiers.
